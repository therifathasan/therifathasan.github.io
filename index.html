The problem

Ordering by experience led to both giveaways and stock-outs

We import 17 Haribo products from Turkey in one shared 40-ft container. Until 2026, quantities were typed onto the supplier's order form based on experience.

The question I set out to answer

How much of each product should go in the next container, so that nothing expires and nothing runs out?

The process

An Excel model that asks four questions for every product

Everything is measured in months of sales, so a 25g pack and a 160g bag can be compared directly.

Approved for the Nov 2026 order
1

Four questions, one worked example

The same four questions run for every product. Goldbears 80g shows how the answers turn into an order.

1How much do we sell in a normal month?The middle value (median) of 19 months of sales, with free giveaways removed.
2How much do we need until the next container?Six months of sales, plus a 10% safety buffer.
3What will still be in stock when it lands?Stock today, plus the order already at sea, minus what sells before March.
4How much can we sell before it expires?Shelf life, minus 4 months at sea, minus 3 months that shops need in hand.

2

Normal monthly sales: using the median, not the average

Monthly sales jump around. Spikes pull an average up, and the business would order too much again. The median, the middle month, ignores both spikes and dips.

One container, 17 products, shared fairly

The must-order amounts filled 71% of the container. The model shares the other 29%: each product gets the same fraction (47%) of its room before the expiry limit.

Expiry limits would allow 132% of a container, more than fits. Sharing the gap evenly means no product is favoured and the box ships full.

The problem

A day-old sales report, and distributor orders based on judgment

The general-trade team relied on a daily Excel report that the sales admin team rebuilt every day from ERP exports. Distributors decided their own primary orders without our demand data.

What went wrong
Gap 1Always a day behind

The team couldn't see today's orders, so they couldn't react during the day.

Gap 2Hours of manual work

Exporting, pasting and checking took a large part of the team's day, every day.

Gap 3Stock-outs at distributors

Popular products ran out, so the sales force couldn't fill shop orders.

Gap 4Overstock of slow lines

Distributors held too much of slow products, tying up money and risking expiry.

The question I set out to answer

How can the team see today's sales and stock, and size each distributor's order on real demand, without anyone rebuilding a report?

The process

A live dashboard built with AI coding tools, checked by me

The AI tools wrote much of the code. The analytical judgement, the metric definitions and the checking were mine.

How the order plan sizes each order
1 · DemandWhat the sales force orderedEach distributor's orders over the last 7, 14 or 30 days. Orders, not deliveries, so stock-outs don't hide demand.
2 · TargetDays of stock to hold7, 14 or 21 days of cover, set by the sales team.
3 · OrderThe gap, in whole cartonsDemand for the target period, minus stock on hand, rounded up to full cartons.
